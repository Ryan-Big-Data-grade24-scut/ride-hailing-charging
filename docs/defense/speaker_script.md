## Slide 1 — Title & team (0:00–0:30)

Good morning. I am Ruixi Liang from Big Data Class 1. Our title is *"When Should an
Electric Ride-Hailing Driver Charge?"* — optimal intra-day charging under stochastic
order demand and time-of-use pricing.

[Point at the one-line statement.] The whole talk in one sentence: we model a single
electric ride-hailing driver's charging and order decisions as a Markov decision process,
and we show that **the cheapest electricity hour is almost never the right hour to charge**.

---

## Slide 2 — Background (0:30–1:45)

The problem is a conflict between two schedules that do not line up.

A ride-hailing driver earns from serving orders. Electricity is a pure cost, and it is
priced by a time-of-use tariff that is about 3.8 times cheaper at night than during the
four-to-seven super-peak. But the order flow peaks in the middle of the day and dies at
four in the morning — so the cheap hours and the busy hours are, in this city, roughly
complementary.

Charging takes a fifteen-minute slot. So the driver has to trade a slot of earning against a
slot of charging, and the value of that trade is not the tariff — it is the **opportunity
cost**, the orders that will never arrive while the car is plugged in.

And the naive rule that both real drivers and naive dispatchers propose — "just charge in
the cheapest hour" — ignores that completely. [Figure: one simulated day.] The blue curve is
demand, the grey is the tariff, and the orange dots are where our optimal policy actually
charges. They are not at the tariff minimum.

---

## Slide 3 — Research question & contributions (1:45–2:30)

So we ask: **when should the driver charge, and how much does charging in the cheapest hour
instead of the optimal one actually cost?**

We deliberately keep the question small and sharp — one driver, one day, one battery —
because that is what allows us to solve it *exactly*, and an exact answer is what lets us
decompose *why* the optimal policy looks the way it does.

Four contributions: a joint MDP of order acceptance and charging with 8,256 states and
analytically summed Poisson arrivals; a decomposition that explains the departure from
the tariff rule; a closed-form multi-day cycle giving the shadow price of stored energy;
and full reproducibility — every number in the paper is regenerated by one script.

---

## Slide 4 — The optimization problem (2:30–3:20)

The decisions are: charge or not in slot t, how many orders to accept in slot t, and the
policy mapping time and state of charge to those two.

The objective is expected net earnings: fare revenue minus the electricity bill.

The constraints are the state-of-charge reserve floor at 15 percent, the energy balance, and
the physical limits of the pack and the charger.

[Point at the right-hand box.] The key trade-off is this: every slot spent charging is a
slot not spent earning — but the value of a slot depends on the *demand profile*, not on the
tariff. Cheap electricity in an empty hour is a loss. Expensive electricity during peak
orders is a smaller loss. That asymmetry is the entire paper.

---

## Slide 5 — Data and assumptions (3:20–4:10)

One public dataset drives demand: the New York City TLC trip records for January 2024 —
2.7 million in-profile trips over 30 full days, from which we derive the arrival rate and
the fare rate.

The vehicle and the tariff are *assumptions*, and we label them as such: a 40 kWh pack,
60 kW charging at 92 percent efficiency, and a four-tier time-of-use tariff.

The honesty line of this paper is on this slide: **the demand profile is measured; the
tariff is assumed.** Section 7 varies the tariff assumption directly, so the reader can see
which conclusions depend on it.

---

## Slide 6 — Method (4:10–5:10)

We use three models, each answering one question that the previous one could not.

The deterministic DP answers: what is the best schedule if the future were known in
advance? It is a lower bound and a sanity check we can verify by hand.

The MDP answers: what should the driver do given only the current state? Orders arrive
stochastically, and the Bellman recursion sums over the Poisson arrivals in closed form —
no sampling, no scenarios.

The cyclic multi-day MDP answers: what is worth carrying into tomorrow? The terminal value
is the value of starting tomorrow, so it is a fixed point, solved by value iteration.

Why not a MILP? It is possible, but stochastic arrivals make the objective non-linear in
the decisions, so we would have to sample scenarios — trading a twenty-minute solve for
fifty percent approximation error. The MDP is exact, and its policy can be read off slot by
slot, which is what makes the key insight visible at all.

---

## Slide 7 — Baseline and comparison (5:10–6:20)

Eight policies, 570 simulated feasible days, and the *same demand realisation* for every
policy so the comparison is paired.

Our MDP earns 946 dollars and 46 cents a day with 95 percent feasibility. The
cheapest-hour rule earns 788 dollars and 21 cents and is feasible on only 11 percent of
days. That is **158 dollars a day, 20 percent**, over a rule that costs nothing to implement.

Against a perfect-forecast deterministic DP the gain is 21 dollars 47 cents, 2.3 percent —
which is the honest measure of what optimisation buys once you remove the advantage that
forecasting would give you.

[Important — do not skip this.] The big number in the literature is the *book value* gap:
the deterministic plan books 1,242 dollars against the MDP's 939. It looks like the price of
uncertainty. It is not. 95 percent of that gap is a Jensen artefact of truncating service at
capacity — E[min(N, C)] is not min(E[N], C) — and after correcting it the gap is 14 dollars,
not 303. The lesson we would defend in the defence: **book value is not executability.**

---

## Slide 8 — Sensitivity and extension (6:20–7:05)

Does the conclusion survive when the assumptions move? Seven one-factor sweeps — tariff
spread, battery size, consumption, charger power, peak utilisation, fare level, and
start-of-day state of charge. The gain is positive everywhere, and it *grows* as the energy
budget tightens.

Ablations A1 to A6 isolate every mechanism. Exactly one ablation changes the policy
qualitatively: removing the opportunity-cost term turns the MDP back into the
cheapest-hour rule. So we know precisely which term is doing the work.

The multi-day cycle gives a shadow price of stored energy of 24 cents per kilowatt-hour,
with an optimal start-of-day state of charge of 36.8 percent.

---

## Slide 9 — The excursion extension (7:05–8:10)

This is our extension, and it comes from the pain point we care about most: **charging is a
trip, not a slot.** You drive to the station, you wait in a queue, you plug in, you drive
back. The naive model charges for the plug time only.

Two things fall out. First, the state space does *not* grow: the queue is exogenous, so it
needs no state of its own — still 8,256 states. Second, the opportunity cost is no longer a
term in the objective at all; it *emerges*, because no fare can be collected while the
driver is away. That is a much stronger claim than "we added a term."

And the numbers are uncomfortable for the whole field. On the 40 kWh car, a two-hour queue
costs between 24 and 79 dollars a day and drops feasibility from 87.6 to 61.9 percent. On
the 57.6 kWh car the same queue costs about zero — a bigger pack lets the driver leave
early and dodge it entirely.

[Land this hard.] The queue is worth up to 79 dollars a day. Optimisation is worth 21.
**The queue costs more than the optimisation.**

---

## Slide 10 — Key insight (8:10–9:05)

This is the figure we would keep if we could only keep one.

The tariff spread across the day is 31 cents per kilowatt-hour. The demand spread over the
same 24 hours is 18.5 times. Opportunity cost dominates by an order of magnitude, so the
optimal policy buys energy where it is cheap *and* charges where the demand gap is small.

The result: 74.4 percent of feasible days contain at least one off-valley charge, and every
single day charges in the deep night. But 25.6 percent of days are valley-only. The modal
charging slot is 04:45, on 87.2 percent of days — and then 20:30 and 12:30 appear, which
are shoulder-tier prices *and* still inside the demand shoulder. They are the only slots
that satisfy both conditions.

The general lesson: **price a time slot by its opportunity cost, not by its sticker price.**

---

## Slide 11 — Conclusion (9:05–10:15)

Seven findings. Let me state the ones that matter.

Charging at the tariff minimum is the worst reasonable rule. The optimal policy is
valley-anchored but not valley-exclusive. The decision is driven by demand availability,
not price. Stored energy is worth 24 cents per kilowatt-hour across a multi-day cycle.
Book value is not executability. Charging is a trip.

And the last one is a boundary condition, and it is the one we would lead with: **whether
this problem is worth solving with an optimiser at all is decided by the hardware, not by
the algorithm.** While usable energy is below 1.1 times the day's consumption, the
optimiser gains under 1 percent over a rule of thumb. Above that, 6 percent.

Our limitations, honestly: a single driver; the tariff is an assumption rather than a
measured price; the queue is exogenous, so a driver's own decision cannot lengthen the
queue he faces. Next: an endogenous queue, a finer charging sub-grid, and multi-agent
congestion.

## Slide 13 — Backup: expected questions (only if asked)

The guide lists eight questions; three have non-trivial answers, and they are on the slide.

*Why is this an optimization problem?* Two coupled decisions compete for the same scarce
resource: the driver's time and battery.

*Why is the reserve constraint necessary?* Without it the DP plans runs that strand the
driver at zero state of charge; we proved the constraint is non-redundant and measured the
resulting infeasibility.

*What if the charger is busy?* We model the queue as exogenous and sweep four shapes and
five levels. An endogenous queue — drivers competing for the same charger — is the next
step, and honestly the most interesting one.

## Anticipated hard questions (prepared answers)

**"Why only one driver? Isn't that unrealistic?"** — The deliverable is a clean, exact
solution of the intra-day problem, which is what makes the decomposition possible. The
single-driver result is also the building block: the fleet-level problem has a
queue-interaction externality, which needs a different model (equilibrium or
mean-field), and we say so in the limitations rather than pretending a single-driver
result scales for free.

**"Your deterministic DP books more than the MDP. Isn't the MDP conservative?"** — No:
the DP books a number it cannot achieve. It fails on 21.7 percent of days because it plans
against the mean and leaves no margin. The corrected gap is 14 dollars, and 95 percent of
the raw gap was a Jensen artefact, not conservatism.

**"The tariff is an assumption — doesn't that undermine everything?"** — It is the reason
for Section 7. The conclusion is that opportunity cost dominates the tariff, so a
different tariff changes the numbers but not the mechanism; the only assumption that
breaks the conclusion is a *flat* tariff, and we show that case too.

**"Could the gain of 6 percent just be noise?"** — Every policy is evaluated on the same
realisation of the demand, so the comparison is paired; and the planner-versus-simulator
gap is within about two standard errors, which bounds the residual implementation error.

**"Why not a MILP / a reinforcement-learning approach?"** — Exactness and interpretability.
The MDP gives a closed-form exact solution and a policy we can read slot by slot, which is
what produced the insight. A policy-gradient learner would be solving a problem we have
already solved exactly, at the price of the explanation.

**"What is the real pain point this solves?"** — Not "the driver pays too much for
electricity". It is that the driver's charging decision is made against a tariff, while the
cost that actually decides it is the revenue he gives up — and the queue makes that worse
than either model suggests. The practical output is a rule that a platform could apply to
tell a driver when to go, and the honest statement of when it is worth building at all.
